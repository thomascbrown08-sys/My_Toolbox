Institutional betrayal

When the harm isn't only what happened, but how the system meant to respond failed to.

Definition

A term from Jennifer Freyd's research: harm caused or deepened by an institution's failure to prevent, respond to, or protect against wrongdoing, when that institution held a duty of care and the person depended on it. It's distinct from an individual's wrongdoing — it names what happens when the system that was supposed to help becomes a second source of injury.

The mechanism

A duty of care existed

The institution held real responsibility and power over the person's situation — a court, a hospital, a church, a treatment program.

The institution failed to respond

Through action or inaction — dismissing a report, protecting its own reputation, or simply not doing what it existed to do.

Dependency made it a double injury

The person needed the very institution that failed them, which is what separates this from a stranger's indifference.

What it produces over time

A hard, often lasting difficulty trusting any institution afterward — courts, hospitals, treatment programs, churches — because the betrayal came specifically from a system built to help, not from an unrelated party. This is frequently more disorienting than the original harm, since there's rarely a clear script for naming an institution's failure the way there is for naming a person's.

Where this shows up

The justice system, medical and mental-health systems, mandated treatment programs, schools, and churches — any setting where a dependent, less-powerful person needed protection or fair process and the system prioritized itself instead.

Related tools

Look up

Every human institution is a fallible copy of something real — a Judge who cannot be bribed, a Physician who does not turn away the report of pain, a Church whose Head does not protect His own reputation at His people's expense. Naming where the copy failed isn't cynicism. It's holding the copy to the standard of the original.
