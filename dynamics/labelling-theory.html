Labelling theory

When a single act becomes the lens through which every future one gets read.

Definition

Drawing on sociologists Howard Becker and Everett Hughes: a label — a diagnosis, a record, a role assigned early in a family — can become someone's master status, the trait that overrides every other true thing about them in how they're perceived. Once that happens, people stop responding to who someone actually is and start responding to the label instead.

The mechanism

One act becomes the master status

A single event, once labeled, can outweigh everything else true about a person in how they're subsequently seen.

Others respond to the label, not the person

The label starts predicting how someone is treated, regardless of who they've actually become since.

The label can be internalized

A person can eventually start organizing their own self-understanding around the same label others assigned.

What it produces over time

A corrosive sense that growth doesn't count — that no matter how long someone has changed, evidence of change isn't believed or even looked for, because the label already answered the question in everyone else's mind before the person walked in the room. Real change made over years can feel invisible against a label formed in a moment.

Where this shows up

Criminal records, psychiatric diagnoses used reductively, family reputations formed in childhood ("the difficult one," "the troubled one"), and church labels applied after a disclosure or a discipline process.
