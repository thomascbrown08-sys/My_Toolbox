Coercive control

Not a single incident — an ongoing apparatus that removes autonomy from ordinary life.

Definition

A term from sociologist Evan Stark's research: control exercised as a system rather than a series of isolated events — isolation from outside support, surveillance of movement and communication, and regulation of small daily decisions that would ordinarily belong to the person alone. The apparatus itself, not any single moment inside it, is the harm.

The mechanism

Isolation

Outside relationships and support get cut off, gradually or directly, until the controlling relationship is the primary remaining source of contact.

Surveillance

Movement, communication, and routine are monitored — sometimes openly, sometimes through routed or filtered contact with others.

Micro-regulation

Control extends to small, ordinary decisions, removing the day-to-day autonomy most people don't think to name as a freedom at all.

What it produces over time

Because coercive control rarely announces itself in a single dramatic incident, people who lived under it often struggle to say what actually happened to them — a list of small restrictions can each sound reasonable in isolation, while the accumulated structure was the real injury. This frequently leads to self-doubt about whether the experience "counts" as something worth naming.

Where this shows up

Domestic relationships most visibly, but the same structure can operate in a parent-child relationship well past childhood, or inside a religious or institutional setting where movement, contact, and decisions are all routed through one controlling party.

Look up

Real authority, as Scripture describes it, protects the freedom of those under it rather than confiscating it — a shepherd leads, he doesn't fence every path. Control that removes a person's ordinary autonomy over their own life was never a legitimate exercise of God-given authority, whatever language was used to describe it.
