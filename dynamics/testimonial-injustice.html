Testimonial injustice

Being disbelieved because of who you are, not because of what you actually said.

Definition

A term from philosopher Miranda Fricker: a specific injustice that happens when someone's account is discounted because of a social identity, label, or history attached to them — not because the account itself is weak or contradicted. The content of what was said is never actually evaluated. The speaker is.

The mechanism

Identity discounts credibility before content is heard

A label — a diagnosis, a record, a role in the family — does the work that evidence should be doing.

The discount is invisible to the discounter

It rarely feels like prejudice from the inside. It feels like reasonable caution or common sense.

The speaker's own trust in their perception erodes

Being disbelieved repeatedly teaches self-doubt about one's own read of events, independent of whether that read was accurate.

What it produces over time

A learned expectation that telling the truth won't be believed — which can lead to over-documenting, over-explaining, going quiet altogether, or eventually doubting one's own memory rather than trusting it. The injustice compounds itself: the more a person is disbelieved, the more tentative and "unreliable-sounding" their account can start to seem, which then gets used as further reason to discount it.

Where this shows up

Medical settings, legal proceedings, family systems, and church discipline processes — anywhere a person's diagnosis, record, age, or assigned family role precedes them into the room.
