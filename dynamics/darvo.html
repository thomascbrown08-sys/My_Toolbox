Dynamic

DARVO

Deny, Attack, Reverse Victim and Offender — a specific, recognizable response to being confronted.

Definition

A term from researcher Jennifer Freyd, naming a predictable sequence: when confronted about wrongdoing, the person denies it happened, attacks the credibility or character of the one raising it, and reverses the roles so that the original wrongdoer ends up occupying the sympathetic, victimized position instead.

The mechanism

Deny

Flat denial, regardless of evidence or consistency with other known facts.

Attack

The conversation shifts entirely onto the accuser's motives, stability, or character.

Reverse victim and offender

By the end of the exchange, the person who raised the concern is the one who appears to owe an apology.

What it produces over time

A well-executed DARVO response is disorienting precisely because it works — it can leave the person who raised a legitimate concern genuinely confused about who did wrong, sometimes for years afterward. Recognizing the pattern by name is often the first thing that interrupts it, since the sequence is hard to sustain once someone can see the three moves happening in real time rather than experiencing them as one confusing blur.

Where this shows up

Confrontations about abuse, institutional wrongdoing, family conflict, and church discipline processes gone wrong — anywhere a person raises a real concern to someone with more social power or credibility than they have.

Look up

Truth doesn't become false because it was denied skillfully, and it doesn't become your fault because someone reversed the roles well. Scripture never asks the wronged party to doubt their own testimony simply because the wrongdoer disputed it with confidence.
