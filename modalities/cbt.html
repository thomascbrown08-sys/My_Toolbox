Modality

Cognitive behavioral therapy

Structured, skills-based, and usually the first thing offered — for good reason.

What it actually is

CBT works from a simple, well-tested observation: your thoughts, your feelings, and your behavior all feed each other, and you can often get more traction changing the loop by starting with the thought than by trying to will the feeling away directly. A thought like I always ruin things produces a feeling, the feeling produces a behavior — withdrawing, over-apologizing, giving up early — and the behavior then seems to confirm the original thought. CBT interrupts that loop at the point where it's most workable: the thought itself, examined rather than simply believed.

What a session actually looks like

Less couch, more collaboration. A typical course involves identifying a specific pattern of thinking that keeps showing up — a distortion like all-or-nothing framing, or assuming you know what someone else is thinking — and then testing it the way you'd test any claim: what's the actual evidence for it, what's the evidence against it, is there a more accurate way to state it. Between sessions there's usually homework — noticing the pattern in real time, trying a small behavioral experiment, tracking what actually happened against what you predicted would happen. It's practical and often faster than people expect, typically measured in weeks and months rather than years.

What it's especially good for

Rigid or catastrophic thought patterns, anxiety that's sustained by avoidance, and situations where a specific distorted prediction is doing a lot of the damage — the double bind's aftermath is a good example, since it often leaves someone predicting danger or punishment in situations that no longer contain either. CBT is very good at testing that prediction against reality, gently and repeatedly, until the prediction updates.

What it doesn't replace

CBT works at the level of thought and behavior, which means it can run into a ceiling with anything held mostly in the body or in a relational wound rather than in belief — a nervous system's trained threat response, for instance, or the specific weight of moral injury, which isn't a distorted thought so much as an accurate one that needs a different kind of resolution. A good therapist will usually recognize when that ceiling has been reached and bring in something else alongside it, rather than pushing the same tool harder.

Look up

There's something quietly biblical about the basic move here — testing a thought against the truth rather than simply believing whatever it insists. Take every thought captive is older than CBT and, in places, more demanding than it, because it doesn't stop at "is this accurate" but asks "is this obedient" — whether a thought is being brought under Christ or simply left to run. CBT can help you notice the thought and question it. What it can't supply is who you're bringing it to once you've caught it. That part is worth doing on purpose, deliberately looking up rather than only looking closer at the thought itself.
