Adaptive Disclosure

The named treatment for moral injury specifically — not a general trauma protocol adapted for it.

What it actually is

Developed by Brett Litz and colleagues, initially with combat veterans, for exactly the gap most trauma treatment leaves open: guilt over something actually done, not fear of something that happened. The structure centers on a guided imaginal disclosure — speaking the transgression aloud, in detail, to an imagined compassionate moral authority, rather than to the therapist directly at first. That structure matters: it separates the act of honest confession from the fear of the therapist's personal judgment.

What a session actually looks like

After the disclosure itself, the work moves toward what a path of repair could actually involve — not vague reassurance, and not endless re-litigating of the event, but a concrete conversation about accountability, what amends are realistically possible, and how to integrate what happened into a life that isn't solely defined by it. It holds two things at once on purpose: the act was genuinely wrong, and the person is not only the worst thing they've done.

What it's especially good for

Guilt or shame tied to an actual transgression — something done, something failed to be prevented, something witnessed and not stopped. It's the right tool when standard trauma treatment leaves the guilt untouched because there was never really a fear response to extinguish in the first place.

What it doesn't replace

It's a relatively specialized protocol, and not every therapist is trained in it specifically — worth asking about directly rather than assuming a general trauma therapist has it. It also isn't built for fear-based trauma without a moral-injury component; using it there would be the wrong tool for the actual wound.

Look up

An imagined compassionate authority is the treatment's necessary workaround for not having access to the real one. You do. The disclosure this protocol asks you to imagine has an actual counterpart — a real hearing, before someone who already knows the full weight of what's being confessed and has already dealt with it definitively, not hypothetically.
