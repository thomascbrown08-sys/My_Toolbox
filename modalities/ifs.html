Internal Family Systems

A confusingly similar name to family systems therapy — and a genuinely different thing.

What it actually is

IFS works from the idea that the mind contains distinguishable "parts" — a protective part, a wounded or exiled part, a managing part — and that healing comes through relating to these parts with curiosity rather than either suppressing them or being consumed by them. It shares a name-adjacent cousin in family systems therapy (Bowen), but the two are unrelated in method: one maps a family across generations, the other maps an internal landscape within one person.

What this actually looks like

A therapist guides attention toward whichever "part" is most active in a given moment — the part that's anxious, the part that's angry, the part that's gone quiet — and works to build a relationship with it rather than immediately trying to eliminate it, on the premise that every part, including the ones causing the most trouble, developed to protect something and can be worked with rather than fought.

What it's especially good for

Internal conflict that feels like being pulled two directions at once — wanting closeness and fearing it in the same moment, for instance — where naming the pull as coming from distinguishable impulses, rather than experiencing it as pure contradiction, can make it more workable.

A note on the language, worth stating plainly

IFS's "parts" language sits at a real distance from a Christian, unified-soul anthropology. Scripture describes internal conflict too — the flesh warring against the Spirit, a divided will — but as one person's struggle, not as literally separate sub-selves living inside a single body. The clinical technique can still be useful as a working metaphor for noticing and addressing competing internal pulls, much the way "the old self and the new self" already functions as a working framework in Scripture itself. It's worth holding "parts" as a description of an experience, not a claim about what a person actually, ultimately is.

What it doesn't replace

It isn't a substitute for family systems therapy's generational lens, despite the similar name, and it isn't the right tool for processing a specific traumatic memory the way EMDR is.
