Modality

Family systems therapy

Naming how a family's patterns repeat across generations — without needing anyone in it to be the villain.

What it actually is

Built largely on Murray Bowen's work, family systems therapy treats the family, not the individual, as the actual unit worth understanding. A person's anxiety, role, or pattern usually makes much more sense once you see the whole system it developed inside — who was over-functioning, who was under-functioning, which roles got assigned early and calcified. Patterns like parentification, codependency, or repetition compulsion are rarely isolated to one person; they're usually one visible piece of a larger, older shape.

What a session actually looks like

Often starts with a genogram — a family map going back a generation or two, marking not just relationships but roles: who kept the peace, who was the scapegoat, who managed whose emotions. The work is less about assigning blame and more about noticing the shape — where anxiety flows, which roles keep getting reassigned to whoever's available, what got inherited without anyone choosing it. Sessions can include other family members directly, or can be done individually while still using the family-systems lens on relationships no longer physically present.

What it's especially good for

Patterns that seem to repeat despite genuine effort to change them — the same relational role showing up in a new setting, the same dynamic with a different person wearing the old part. It's also unusually good at removing the sense that you're the only one carrying a pattern, once you see it running two or three generations back.

What it doesn't replace

It's not primarily built for processing a specific traumatic memory — that's EMDR's job — and it doesn't, by itself, address the felt, physical layer that somatic work reaches. It's best understood as a lens for the shape of the whole pattern, usually paired with something that works more directly on the individual pieces inside it.

Where you might meet this

Not to be confused with Internal Family Systems — a similar name for an unrelated method that maps a person's internal landscape rather than a family across generations.

Look up

Honoring father and mother was never the same command as pretending their patterns were healthy. Naming a generational pattern honestly is not dishonor — it's the necessary first step to actually being the one where, by grace, the pattern stops rather than simply repeats one more time under a different name.
