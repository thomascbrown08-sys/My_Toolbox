Modality

EMDR

Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing — a specific protocol for a specific memory, not talk therapy.

What it actually is

The theory, still debated in its particulars but well-supported in its outcomes, is that some memories get stored in a way that keeps them feeling present rather than past — the body still reacts as though the danger is current. EMDR pairs brief, focused recall of a target memory with bilateral stimulation, usually guided eye movements, sometimes tapping or alternating tones. Something about that combination appears to help the brain finish processing the memory into something that feels like it happened, rather than something that's still happening.

What a session actually looks like

It doesn't start with the memory. Early sessions build stabilization and coping resources first, so there's somewhere safe to return to during processing. When the actual reprocessing begins, you're not asked to narrate the whole story in detail the way you might in other trauma therapies — you hold the memory briefly in mind, along with the belief attached to it and the physical sensation that comes with it, while following the bilateral movement. The therapist checks in frequently, and sessions are paced so the memory is approached in a way you can tolerate, not flooded all at once.

What it's especially good for

A specific, identifiable memory that still carries a disproportionate charge — a moment that, when it comes to mind, still produces the original fear or shame at close to full strength, even though you know intellectually it's over. It tends to reach material that talking alone hasn't fully touched, because so much of what got stored is bodily and implicit rather than verbal.

What it doesn't replace

EMDR works best with a clear target. Diffuse, chronic patterns without one specific memory to anchor to — an atmosphere of childhood rather than a single incident — usually need a broader, longer treatment plan first, with EMDR woven in for specific memories once stabilization is solid. It's rarely the whole plan by itself for anything more complex than a single-incident trauma.

Look up

There's something fitting about a memory needing to finish being carried rather than simply being willed away — Scripture never asks you to pretend something didn't happen in order to be free of it. What EMDR offers the nervous system, remembrance before God offers the whole person: the past named accurately, brought into the light, and no longer treated as a live threat to brace against.
