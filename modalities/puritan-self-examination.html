Puritan self-examination

A daily reckoning with the heart, from writers who took the practice seriously enough to also warn against doing it wrong.

What it actually is

Richard Baxter, John Owen, and others wrote extensively about examining the heart's actual state — not generic journaling, but a disciplined look at where affections are actually running, what's being loved and trusted, where sin is at work beneath the surface behavior. It answers the same felt need contemplative journaling or an Examen practice answers, but drawn from a tradition already fluent in exactly this territory, including its dangers.

What this actually looks like

Typically a set time, often evening, spent honestly reviewing the day — not a general mood check, but specific questions: where did love for God show itself today, where did it not, what ruled the heart in a hard moment. Baxter's own counsel is worth carrying into the practice directly: examine with real honesty, but don't let examination become endless. Some seasons call for less self-examination, not more, particularly for a heart already prone to circling.

What it's especially good for

Building an honest, ongoing awareness of the heart's actual state rather than its performed one — useful for anyone whose faith has become more habit than attention. It's also a direct, trusted alternative for someone who wants a contemplative daily practice without importing one from a tradition that needs separate vetting.

What it doesn't replace — the caution, stated plainly

This practice carries real risk for exactly the kind of heart it's most attractive to. If examination stops producing confession, rest, or concrete change, and instead just circles — the same failure revisited without resolution — the practice has become the illness rather than the cure. That's morbid introspection, not godly self-examination, and the same writers who commended the practice were equally clear that it can be overdone. If it's not producing movement, the right response is less examination, not more discipline at it.

Look up

Self-examination was always meant to end somewhere — in confession, in rest, in a heart more attentive to God, not more anxious about itself. A practice that only ever looks inward, and never up, has quietly stopped being what it was designed to be.
