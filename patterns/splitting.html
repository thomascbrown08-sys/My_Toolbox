Pattern

Splitting

Sorting people — or yourself — into wholly good or wholly bad, with no working middle category.

Definition

Splitting is the difficulty holding a mixed, accurate picture of someone — good qualities and real flaws, present at the same time, in the same person. Instead, the mind resolves the tension by sorting: this person is entirely trustworthy, or they've just proven entirely untrustworthy. A single disappointment can flip the whole category. The same sorting often happens toward the self — entirely fine, or a complete failure, with the middle ground unavailable.

The mechanism

Ambiguity feels unsafe

Holding "good and flawed at once" requires tolerating uncertainty about whether someone can be trusted — which, for a nervous system trained by real danger, can feel like an unaffordable risk.

Sorting resolves the tension fast

A clean verdict — good or bad — is more bearable than an unresolved, mixed picture, even when the mixed picture is more accurate.

The verdict can flip entirely

One disappointing moment can move a person from the "good" category to the "bad" one instantly, rather than simply adding a data point to a complex picture.

What it produces over time

Relationships that swing between idealization and disillusionment, since neither extreme requires the harder work of holding a real, complicated person in mind. It also produces harsh self-judgment after ordinary mistakes, since the self gets sorted the same binary way — one failure and the whole "good" verdict flips.

Where this shows up

Common after early relationships where safety genuinely did depend on correctly and quickly reading whether someone was safe or dangerous in the moment — a skill that made sense under real threat and generalizes poorly to ordinary, mixed relationships later.
