Pattern

Moral injury

The wound of having actually done wrong — a different injury than fear-based trauma, and treated differently.

Definition

Most trauma treatment is built around fear — a threat to survival that the body is still braced against. Moral injury is a different wound: the damage done when a person participates in, fails to prevent, or witnesses something that violates their own deeply held moral code. The central feeling isn't fear. It's shame, self-condemnation, and a loss of trust in one's own moral standing.

The mechanism

Transgression, not threat

The nervous system isn't bracing against danger. It's responding to a genuine violation of conscience.

Self-directed condemnation

Unlike fear-based trauma, the "threat" here is often the person's own judgment of themselves, which doesn't fade the way fear extinguishes.

Isolation as the default

Shame drives concealment. The instinct is to carry it alone, which is usually the opposite of what resolves it.

What it produces over time

Standard fear-extinction approaches to trauma often don't fully reach this, because there's no fear response to extinguish — the guilt can be accurate, not distorted, which makes it resistant to the usual "test the thought against reality" move. Left unaddressed, it tends toward self-punishment that looks like conscience but functions more like an unpayable, endlessly re-billed debt.

Where this shows up

Combat and first-responder work are where the clinical literature developed, but the mechanism isn't limited to those settings — any situation where someone acted against their own conscience, failed to act when they believe they should have, or was complicit in harm can produce it.

Look up

This is the one pattern in this toolkit where the theology isn't alongside the treatment — it's structurally doing something clinical technique alone has no resource for. The debt named here is real. It was also, genuinely, already carried somewhere: a garden, a cup, a cross that didn't skip the weight in order to remove it.
